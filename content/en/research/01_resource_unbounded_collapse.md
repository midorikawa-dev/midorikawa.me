## Abstract

This note examines a minimal model of colony collapse without resource shortage, using an artificial rat colony.

It is not a quantitative reproduction of Calhoun's Universe 25 experiment. The narrower question is whether a colony's reproductive and social life cycle can break down even when food and water remain available.

In this model, collapse did not first appear as a decline in headcount. What failed first was transmission: the pathway by which social competence is passed to the next generation.

The result should not be read as a direct explanation of real mice, rats, or human society. It is a way to make one hypothesis executable: that the limiting factor for a colony may be not only resources, but also roles and transmission.

Keywords: Universe 25, social roles, life cycle, social learning, transmission, behavioral sink

## Question

Why would a colony collapse when resources are sufficient?

Population collapse is usually explained through shortage. Food runs out. Water runs out. Space runs out. Predation and disease reduce the population.

Universe 25 is unsettling because many of those pressures were removed. Food and water were available, predators were absent, and the population lived in a closed environment from which it could not disperse. The colony grew, stagnated, and collapsed.

This note does not try to reproduce that historical case as a whole. Calhoun's experiment was a single case, and its interpretation remains debated. The target here is more limited.

Can the finite capacity of social roles break the reproductive and social life cycle, even without resource shortage?

Here, social roles mean the places and relationships required for territory holding, courtship, parenting, and social learning from adults.

## Problem With The First Model

The first model also collapsed. Even with unlimited food and water, the population grew, became overcrowded, births fell, and the colony went extinct.

But that model had a problem. Some post-collapse states were classified by working backward from the outcome. Individuals under high density stress were treated as non-reproductive, and maternal-collapse or beautiful-ones-like states were attached through thresholds.

That can be useful as a diagram of a hypothesis. It is weaker as a test of emergence. If collapse is written into the code, collapse will appear.

The model therefore had to be rebuilt. The question was whether a similar structure would still appear after removing the collapse label and letting it arise from the life process of individuals.

## Life-Cycle Model

Each individual was given a reproductive and social life cycle: sexual maturation, territory acquisition, courtship, mating, pregnancy, birth, parenting, and offspring social learning.

Failure paths were included as well. An individual may fail to acquire territory, fail at courtship, become isolated, lose reproductive ability through chronic stress, or fail to learn social competence when competent adults are absent.

The important point is that this life cycle does not collapse by itself. When social roles are abundant, the population rises, reaches equilibrium, and social competence is maintained across generations. Pathological non-reproductive individuals do not accumulate in any substantial number.

In other words, the model is not broken from the start. After confirming that it can run in a healthy condition, the only intervention is to make social roles finite.

## Intervention

The intervention is small. Food and water remain unlimited. What changes is the capacity of social roles required for territory, courtship, parenting, and social learning.

When social roles are sufficient, the life cycle closes. When they are insufficient, transitions in the life cycle begin to jam.

The experiment asks whether that difference is enough to determine collapse.

## Result

When social roles were made finite, the colony collapsed.

More individuals failed to acquire territory. Courtship and parenting became unstable. Competent adults decreased. Offspring failed to learn social competence, and the next generation of adults became less competent still.

This loop progresses while the population is still large. From the outside, the colony still looks substantial. Inside, however, the functions required to close the life cycle are being lost.

Individuals accumulated in an absorbing state called WITHDRAWN. This resembles Calhoun's beautiful ones, but it was not attached as an outcome label. It appeared from the accumulation of failures in territory, courtship, social learning, and stress.

In the comparison condition, colonies with abundant social roles survived. Colonies with finite social roles collapsed even though food and water were unlimited.

In this model, social-role saturation functioned as a condition for collapse independent of resource shortage.

## The Food-Restriction Paradox

When food was restricted, collapse was suppressed.

This is counterintuitive. Less food should make survival harder.

But food restriction keeps the population lower. Growth stops before the colony greatly exceeds the capacity of its social roles. As a result, the population is less likely to reach the density at which the life cycle breaks across the board.

Unlimited food is therefore not simply a better condition. It removes density-lowering forces such as predation, starvation, and dispersal, exposing a social limit that was otherwise hidden.

In that sense, abundance can be both protective and collapse-enabling.

## From Parameter To Apparatus

Next, the number of social roles was no longer given as an abstract parameter. In Universe 25, role capacity emerges from the geometry of the apparatus.

The simplified apparatus has a closed enclosure, defensible nests along the perimeter, an open central floor, and a boundary that prevents dispersal.

The capacity of social roles was determined by which individuals could occupy and defend which nests. The individual life-cycle rules were left unchanged.

Individuals unable to secure nests accumulated in the center. The space was not completely full, but socially usable places were insufficient.

Sweeping the number of nest boxes separated apparatus sizes that tended to collapse from those that tended to persist. In this model, collapse became likely below roughly 32 nest boxes, while 96 and above remained stable.

This value is not a direct estimate for reality. It is a critical capacity inside the model.

The implication is still clear. Carrying capacity is not determined only by food. It also depends on the capacity of social roles: who can reproduce where, and from whom the young can learn.

## Interpretation

What collapsed in this model was not headcount at first.

What collapsed was transmission.

The population remains for a while. But competent adults decrease. Offspring fail to learn. Those offspring cannot become the next generation of teachers.

Once this loop begins, recovery is difficult even after density falls. By the time density is low again, the teachers are already gone.

This irreversibility turns the process from ordinary population decline into social collapse.

## Limitations

This model does not reproduce the full behavior of real mice or rats. Movement, sensation, learning, and reproduction are all abstracted. It does not claim quantitative agreement with Calhoun's experimental data.

The interpretation of Universe 25 itself is also debated. This note does not settle that debate.

It treats one hypothesis: in a closed system where resource constraints are removed, saturation of social roles can break developmental social learning and the reproductive life cycle.

The model makes that hypothesis executable.

## Next Question

If the core of collapse is a break in transmission, the next question becomes clear.

What reconnects that broken transmission?

Is parental investment enough? Is kin-directed altruism enough? Can costly behavior toward unrelated offspring prevent collapse?

The next note defines love not as an emotion, but as a set of behaviors that support the colony: social buffering, mentorship, alloparenting, and adoption. The question is whether those behaviors change the critical conditions of the behavioral sink.

## Notes

1. Universe 25: John B. Calhoun's experiment on mouse population collapse in a resource-rich closed environment.
2. beautiful ones: Calhoun's term for individuals reported to avoid fighting and reproduction, spending much of their behavior on self-maintenance such as grooming.
3. carrying capacity: The upper limit of population size or activity that an environment can maintain. It is often discussed in terms of food or space; here, the capacity of social roles is also treated as a limiting factor.
